Football Fortresses – ClubElo-Adjusted Home Advantage in Europe’s Top 5 Leagues

Paper PDFVideo Explanation

Some football grounds are famous for being difficult places to visit: Manchester United’s Old Trafford, Real Madrid’s Santiago Bernabéu, and Bayern Munich’s Allianz Arena are often called fortresses because of their reputation as places where visiting teams struggle to win. But do these clubs have unusually large home advantages, or do they simply win more at home because they are stronger teams?

This study explores that question across Europe’s top five leagues. It accounts for team strength, estimates each club’s home advantage, and compares it with the usual home advantage in the same league and season. The aim is to see which clubs stand out and whether that extra advantage lasts. Even a small edge can matter when a few points decide a title, European qualification, or relegation.

  1. 01

    01 / The Data

    Start with the Matches and Team Ratings

    We assemble match information from Football-Data.co.uk, which provides data for Europe’s top five leagues across many seasons. We combine the separate league and season files into one dataset for the analysis, covering 2008/09 through 2025/26 in England’s Premier League, Germany’s Bundesliga, Italy’s Serie A, Spain’s La Liga, and France’s Ligue 1.

    We also bring in ratings from ClubElo.[1]Historical ClubElo DataWhen this report was prepared, ClubElo’s data download service, called an API, was having access problems. The public charts available at the time covered only about four years. Older ratings came from SportSignal, an archive that had saved ClubElo records going back to 2008. These describe each team’s strength based on its previous results, with a higher rating suggesting a stronger team. Under the Elo system, beating a strong opponent generally earns more rating points than beating a weaker one, but a higher rating never guarantees a win.

    We then join the match records with the ClubElo ratings to create the combined dataset used in the study. This gives each game both its recorded result and a measure of how strong the two teams were before they played.[2]Match the Ratings Before KickoffEach match is joined with both teams’ Elo ratings valid on the calendar day before kickoff. This keeps the result being studied from changing the rating used to explain it.

    Which Games Are We Analyzing?

    The merged file initially contains 34,371 matches across five leagues, beginning in 2007/08 and ending in 2025/26. We leave out 2007/08 because some historical Elo ratings are missing, so the analysis starts in 2008/09. We also exclude 2019/20 and 2020/21 because pandemic restrictions prevented fans from attending many games and disrupted the usual home and away conditions.[3]Why Exclude the Pandemic Seasons?The 2019/20 season was disrupted partway through. In leagues that resumed, two clubs could have faced each other once with fans and once without them, making the home and away conditions difficult to compare. Ligue 1 ended the season without playing its remaining matches. Attendance rules also changed during 2020/21. For example, the Premier League was mostly played without fans, with limited returns in December and near the end of the season. Both seasons are excluded to keep the comparison focused on more typical home conditions.

    Starting in 2008/09 and removing the two pandemic seasons leaves 28,994 matches. The French clubs Saint-Étienne and Arles also have incomplete Elo records. Removing all 526 matches involving either club leaves 28,468 matches across 16 seasons. This keeps the schedule complete among the remaining teams, so each club faces the same opponents twice in a season, once at home and once away.

    Results awarded by the football authorities stay as recorded in the source files. Each game is counted once. Not every club appears in every season, since teams move between divisions through promotion and relegation.

  2. 02

    02 / The Match Measures

    Record the Score Difference and Rating Gap

    For each match in the dataset, the away team’s goals are subtracted from the home team’s goals. This shows how the home team performed: whether it won, lost, or drew, and by how many goals.

    The home and away teams’ Elo ratings are also compared for each match. Subtracting the away team’s rating from the home team’s rating shows which side was rated stronger before kickoff and how large that difference was. This provides a way to account for the strength of the two teams when looking at the result.

  3. 03

    03 / The Conversion

    Learn How Rating Points Relate to Goals

    Knowing that one team has a rating advantage of 100 points does not yet tell us what that means for the score. The next step uses the match results to estimate how differences in Elo ratings relate to differences in goals. This creates a conversion that can be used to allow for team strength in each match.

    At this stage, Y and d have already been calculated for every match. OLS uses those known values to estimate β̂, which converts rating gaps into goals, and α̂, the starting level for each season. Those estimates can then be used to predict a goal difference. They do not change Y, which remains the difference in the actual score. The final term in the equation represents what the relationship does not explain.

    The calculation is carried out separately for each league using all 16 seasons kept in the study. Each league gets one conversion from Elo points into goals, while the usual home goal difference between equally rated teams can change each season. These values are chosen using ordinary least squares, or OLS. This method compares the predictions with the actual results and makes the squared prediction errors as small as possible, giving larger misses more weight.

    The last column shows each league’s usual home advantage averaged across the 16 seasons, calculated later in the analysis. It ranges from about 0.304 goals in Serie A to 0.431 in La Liga. These differences have not been tested well enough to conclude that the leagues truly differ.

  4. 04

    04 / The Adjustment

    Allow for the Difference in Team Strength

    Once the rating gap has been converted into goals, that amount is subtracted from each match’s actual goal difference. This adjusts the result for the strength difference between the teams before they played. The number left over can then be used to investigate home advantage, while still allowing for other influences on the result.

    This calculation removes only the part associated with the rating gap. The season’s starting level is deliberately kept because it includes the usual benefit of playing at home, which the next step needs to measure. As a result, R is not the same as the leftover error in Equation 3.

  5. 05

    05 / The Club Effects

    Estimate What Each Club Gains from Playing at Home

    The Elo adjustment gives a more comparable starting point, but it may still miss some differences in team strength. A club could continue to perform well both at home and away because it is a strong team. The next calculation uses both sets of results to separate that remaining strength from the additional performance associated with playing at home.

    Here, R is already known from Equation 4. OLS uses those adjusted match results to estimate one â and one ĥ for every club in that league and season. The â value describes remaining strength at both home and away matches. The ĥ value describes the additional advantage when the club hosts. Both are estimated together, while the earlier Elo conversion stays unchanged.

    The remaining strength values describe how teams compare with one another, so they need a shared starting point. Adding the same number to every team’s value would leave all the differences between teams unchanged. Setting their average to zero fixes that starting point and gives one consistent set of strength values.

    This does not make the teams equally strong. Some strength values can be positive and others negative. Setting their average to zero simply puts them on the same measuring scale.

    The calculation also measures how uncertain the estimates are. It uses a method called HC3, which allows some matches to have more unpredictable results than others. Step 9 explains how that uncertainty is presented.

  6. 06

    06 / The Comparison

    Compare Each Club with Its League Average

    A club can benefit from playing at home without having an unusual home advantage. To see whether it stands out, its estimated home advantage needs to be compared with what is typical in its own league that season. The first calculation adds the clubs’ estimated home advantages and divides by the number of clubs to find that average.

    The paper calls this average the league home baseline. Equation 7 averages the home advantage estimates obtained for the clubs in Equation 5. Every club kept in the study has the same weight, including the club being compared. Subtracting this average from a club’s own home advantage then shows whether that club gains more or less from hosting than its domestic rivals.

    Suppose a club’s estimated home advantage is 0.60 goals and the league average is 0.35. Its extra advantage is 0.25 goals per home game. This difference is called relative home advantage. Positive means above the league average, negative means below it, and zero means equal to it.

    Below average does not necessarily mean playing at home hurts the club. A club can benefit from being at home and still benefit less than its rivals. These values describe average differences across games, not a fraction of a goal added to every score.

    After these calculations, the pieces can be put back together to predict the home goal difference for a match. The prediction combines the part associated with Elo ratings, the remaining difference in team strength, the league’s usual home advantage, and the home club’s advantage above or below that average.

    The league’s usual home advantage plus the club’s extra advantage gives the club’s total home effect. Splitting it into those two pieces changes how the result is explained, not the prediction itself. In France, the league average covers only the clubs left in the study.

  7. 07

    07 / The Cross Check

    Check the Answer Using Seasonal Totals

    The next step checks whether a different calculation produces the same answers. Because every remaining team plays every other team once at home and once away, the adjusted results can be added up across the season to recover the home advantages and remaining strength values. Agreement between these totals and the earlier model helps check that the calculations have been implemented correctly.

    These checks are carried out within one league and one season at a time. To keep the formulas easier to read, the league and season labels are left off. N still means the number of teams kept in that particular league and season.

    The away total needs a sign change because R always starts from the home team’s point of view. If a visiting team wins 1–0, the home goal difference is −1. Reversing that sign records a positive result for the visiting club. The same idea applies after the Elo adjustment.

    With N teams, there are N(N − 1) matches in the complete schedule. Averaging the adjusted margins across those games should recover the same league home average as Equation 7. Team strength differences cancel across the schedule because every team appears equally often at home and away.

    The club’s adjusted home and away totals are then combined with the league average to recover its home advantage. That home advantage is also used to recover the club’s remaining strength. With a complete schedule and more than two teams, these calculations reproduce the earlier model’s results, with any differences small enough to be explained by computer rounding.

    Getting the same answer two ways helps check the code and calculations. It does not prove that crowds or stadiums caused the estimated advantage.

  8. 08

    08 / The Interpretation

    Know What the Estimates Can Tell Us

    The estimated home advantage describes the part of a club’s home performance left after allowing for the strength of the teams. Several things could contribute to it, including travel, familiarity with the ground, the crowd, and unpredictable results. The calculation does not separate these explanations, so the estimate cannot be attributed to the stadium or crowd alone.

    Elo ratings also come from previous results, which may already reflect earlier benefits of playing at home. The estimates therefore describe home advantage after allowing for this particular measure of team strength.

    Each seasonal estimate is based on relatively few home games. Premier League, La Liga, and Serie A clubs usually have 19, while Bundesliga clubs have 17. The remaining French clubs have between 16 and 19. A few surprising results can have a noticeable influence on one season’s estimate.

  9. 09

    09 / The Uncertainty

    Show How Uncertain Each Estimate Is

    Each home advantage is estimated from a limited number of games. If some of those results had turned out differently, the estimate could change too. To show how much uncertainty surrounds the answer, the study calculates a range around each estimate of relative home advantage. This range is called a confidence interval.

    The width of the range depends on a standard error, a measure of uncertainty in the estimate. A larger standard error means a wider range and a less precise answer. Since relative home advantage subtracts the league average from the club’s own estimate, the calculation accounts for uncertainty in both numbers and how they move together. HC3 is the method used to calculate this uncertainty.

    An interval entirely above zero is preliminary evidence of an above average seasonal advantage. An interval entirely below zero points the other way. If the interval includes zero, the results do not clearly distinguish the club from the league average. That does not prove the club has no extra advantage.

    The “95%” describes the method used to build the interval. Under its assumptions, repeating the study with new samples would produce intervals containing the actual effect about 95% of the time. It does not assign a 95% probability to the effect being inside this particular interval.

    Two limits remain. The intervals treat the earlier Elo conversion as if it were known exactly, leaving out the uncertainty in estimating it. They also do not account for checking many clubs and seasons. With enough comparisons, some intervals will miss zero by chance. Both issues need further work.

    10 / The Club Comparison

    See Why Home Wins Are Only Part of the Story

    Dortmund and Manchester United provide a useful example because both appear in all 16 seasons kept in the study. Their seasonal estimates can therefore be averaged over the same years, with each season receiving equal weight. This makes the time periods comparable, although the clubs still play different opponents in different leagues.

    Dortmund’s average extra home advantage is about 0.207 goals per match larger than United’s. The values are rounded in the table, so they may not subtract exactly. This comparison describes the estimates. It has not established that the difference is too large to be explained by chance.

    The gap also depends heavily on individual seasons. In 2022/23, the clubs’ relative estimates are nearly identical at about 1.19 goals. In 2024/25, Dortmund’s is 1.706 and United’s is −0.255. Removing that one season reduces their historical average gap from 0.207 to about 0.090 goals. Dortmund has the higher estimate in 10 of the 16 seasons, but that alone does not establish a lasting advantage.

    United’s 2012/13 title season shows why wins and home advantage are different. United earned 48 points from 16 home victories. Yet its estimated home effect was about 0.182 goals against a league average of 0.323, leaving a relative estimate of −0.141. The uncertainty interval includes zero, so this is not clear evidence that United’s actual advantage was below average. A strong team can win frequently at home without receiving an unusually large extra benefit from hosting.

  11. 11

    11 / The Rankings

    Read the Rankings and Charts with Care

    Once each club has an estimate, the clubs can be ranked within their league and season. There are 1,548 club seasons in total. Ranking the total home advantages or the relative home advantages gives the same order, because the same league average is subtracted from every club. The ranking shows which estimates are larger, but does not establish that neighbouring clubs are genuinely different.

    Only 35 seasonal estimates have approximate 95% intervals entirely above zero. That count still needs the cautions from Step 9 about uncertainty in the Elo conversion and the chance findings that arise from making many comparisons.

    The historical charts average each club’s relative advantage across its available seasons. Each season counts equally, and a club must appear in at least six seasons to be included. That is a provisional way to avoid ranking clubs on very short histories. It does not make every average equally reliable, and some clubs are being compared over different years.

    The distribution charts show how spread out the seasonal estimates are. Relative advantages average zero within each league and season because the league average was subtracted. The interesting question in those charts is how far estimates spread on either side of zero.

    In each box plot, the box holds the middle half of the estimates and the line inside marks the middle value. The lines extending from the box reach the most extreme values within one and a half box widths beyond either edge. More distant values appear as separate dots. This describes the spread of estimates, not a confidence interval for one club. A wider spread may reflect less reliable estimates as well as real differences.

  12. 12

    12 / The Time Comparison

    Look for a Connection Between Seasons

    If a club has a lasting extra advantage at home, a high estimate in one season might be followed by a high estimate in the next. To explore this, each club’s relative home advantage is paired with its estimate exactly one season later, as long as it stays in the same league. This produces 1,160 pairs. The comparison does not jump across missing seasons, relegation gaps, or the excluded pandemic years.

    The paper’s chart places the earlier estimate from left to right and the following estimate from bottom to top. The diagonal line marks the same estimate in both years. Points can be above average in both years, below average in both, or switch sides of their domestic average.

    Arsenal

    From 2017/18 to 2018/19, total points rose from 63 to 70. Home points slipped from 47 to 45, while away points rose from 16 to 25. Home goal difference fell from +34 to +26, while away goal difference improved from −11 to −4. The team improved overall, but the contrast between home and away became smaller.

    Manchester United

    From 2012/13 to 2013/14, United fell from first place with 89 points to seventh with 64. Home goal difference fell from +26 to +8, while away goal difference fell only from +17 to +13. The sharper decline at home fits with a lower relative home estimate.

    Dortmund

    From 2023/24 to 2024/25, total points fell from 63 to 57. Home goal difference improved from +15 to +25, while away goal difference fell from +10 to −5. The team’s overall points declined, but its contrast between home and away grew stronger.

    Real Madrid

    From 2015/16 to its title winning 2016/17 season, points rose from 90 to 93. Home goal difference fell from +54 to +28, while away goal difference rose from +22 to +37. Overall success improved while the relative home estimate fell.

    These examples help explain why overall success and extra home advantage can move in different directions. They are not a complete explanation of the estimates, which also account for ratings, remaining strength, and league averages. A club can appear in several pairs, and the chart does not show uncertainty around each estimate. It cannot establish a lasting advantage by itself.

  13. 13

    13 / The Next Steps

    Separate What Is Known from What Comes Next

    The analysis so far puts the matches on a consistent basis, estimates how much clubs benefit from playing at home, and checks those estimates using seasonal totals. It also compares clubs and follows their estimates across seasons. The results show differences, but more work is needed to tell whether those differences represent lasting home advantages. The next steps focus on three areas.

    Check What Elo Adds

    Repeat the seasonal comparison without Elo, using exactly the same games. This would show how much the rating adjustment changes the results. Another proposed check would estimate the rating conversion and club effects together, rather than one stage at a time. These checks have not yet been completed.

    Look Across Several Seasons

    Combining three or five seasons might help reduce the influence of unusual results. But it would also assume that home advantage stays more similar during that period. Other methods may better allow the advantage to change and account for clubs with different amounts of data. The choice is still open.

    Make the Uncertainty More Complete

    The analysis needs to include uncertainty from the Elo conversion and allow for the many club comparisons being made. More seasons, information about possible explanations, and a closer look at awarded results could also help. The current estimates describe patterns after accounting for team strength. They do not prove that a particular stadium causes those patterns.

  14. 14

    14 / The Research Process

    How the Research Question Changed

    The project originally looked at whether betting markets continued to favour prominent clubs after poor performances. Early checks showed that bookmaker odds predicted match results better than the Elo comparison being used. They also raised a problem with interpreting surprises. An unexpected score could reflect an unpredictable result rather than evidence that a team played much better or worse than expected.

    Reading earlier research and discussing the project with Dr. Craig Depken II helped redirect the study toward home advantage. The revised question offered a clearer way to separate a team’s strength from its performance at home.
